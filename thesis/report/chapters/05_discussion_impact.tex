\chapter{Discussion and Project Impact}
\label{ch:discussion}

The earlier chapters explained what SmartLoad is, how it is built, and how it
behaves under test. This chapter weighs it. It reads what the results in
\cref{ch:impl_testing} show, identifies where the system is strong and which design
choices were deliberate trade-offs, and traces the effects a system in the request
path carries beyond the laboratory across four contexts: economic, societal,
environmental, and cultural. The framing is the one the project has held throughout:
explain the reasoning behind each decision and let that judgment stand on its own.

\section{Discussion of Performance}
\label{disc:performance}

Does SmartLoad work? The answer has layers. The \emph{control loop} works end to
end: observation, decision, and actuation form an unbroken chain, and every
component has a Phase-1 run loop wired and enabled with a deterministic fallback
beneath it. The \emph{learned components} inside that loop sit at three stages of
maturity, and the deterministic baseline behind each one is what makes shipping in
that mixed state sound. This section separates what the mechanism demonstrably does
from what the current trained models add on top.

\Cref{box:disc:mechanism} draws the line the chapter defends: the closed control
loop is demonstrated against a live stack, while the current trained routing model
only matches its classical baseline on the workload it was tested against. The first
is the engineering result the design set out to prove; the second is an honest report
on a single learned component within it.

\subsection{Strengths}
\label{disc:strengths}

\paragraph{Closed-loop proactive autoscaling is demonstrated end to end.} The
forecast-to-capacity loop closes against a live stack. In the committed adaptive
benchmark run shown in \cref{fig:imc:pool}, a forecast-driven burst scaled the
backend pool up toward its single-host budget (\texttt{max\_backends}$=5$) and then
shrank it back as the burst subsided, with time-to-react between actions on the order
of seconds rather than minutes (\cref{fig:imc:react}).
% TODO(vps-rerun): verified pool range + scaling-action count from fresh VPS adaptive batch
This is the affirmative answer to the project's autoscaling research question: the
pool grew ahead of modelled demand and contracted when demand receded, the
bidirectional, proactive behaviour the design is built around. The run is no lucky
first pass but the product of a harness built to drive exactly that behaviour over
the full forecast-to-capacity path.

\paragraph{The deployed anomaly engine catches gradual degradation the stateless rules miss.}
The shipped anomaly default is the deterministic \texttt{trend\_rule} engine, a
stateful detector that tracks the direction of the signal rather than a single
threshold crossing. On the gradual-degradation profile it reaches an $F_1$ of
\BManFone{} (recall \BManRecall{}, precision \BManPrecision{}), while every stateless
engine, including the plain threshold rule and both Isolation Forest variants, scores
$F_1=0.000$ on the same slow drift. That jump from $0.000$ to \BManFone{} is the real
anomaly contribution: a creeping degradation that never trips a fixed threshold is
exactly what a direction-aware rule is built to catch. The artefact-free
\texttt{threshold} rule remains the fallback if no learned artefact is present.

A trained Isolation Forest is available as a \emph{selectable} alternative. On its own
training-distribution holdout it scores an $F_1$ of \BMifFone{}, and a separate sweep
comparing the \texttt{threshold} rule against the Isolation Forest (excluding the
deployed \texttt{trend\_rule}) finds the two agree on about \BManAgree{} of the
operating-range grid. Every disagreement runs one way, the threshold rule reporting
healthy where the Isolation Forest reports unhealthy, so the Isolation Forest
over-reacts rather than under-reacts. Keeping a more aggressive detector behind the
same contract and measuring it against the baseline across the whole range is what
lets an operator switch it on deliberately. The three numbers describe three
different things: \BManFone{} is the deployed \texttt{trend\_rule} on gradual
degradation, \BMifFone{} is the Isolation Forest's own holdout, and \BManAgree{} is
the threshold-versus-Isolation-Forest sweep that excludes the deployed engine.

\paragraph{The data and control planes are mechanically sound.} Beneath the learned
engines, the plumbing is verified rather than assumed. Per-request fidelity is
asserted at every layer of the telemetry path; the sidecar consumes decision
envelopes across four channels and atomically rewrites the NGINX upstream
configuration; and the autoscaler's actuation, audit, and reactive-fallback paths are
all exercised under test. The harness reproduced the expected mechanical signatures,
including a baseline $p_{95}$ that rose substantially once backend heterogeneity was
introduced.
% TODO(vps-rerun): exact baseline p95 figures from the fresh batch
That is the slowdown the design predicts when one slow backend receives its share of
round-robin traffic, an independent confirmation that the measurement apparatus
reports what it should.

\paragraph{Clean operator surfaces, a complete audit trail, and broad test
coverage.} The system is operable, not merely runnable. The operator interface ships
a multi-view console backed by real endpoints, a consolidated one-call status read
across all services, and a live decision feed that merges all four decision-plane
channels. Every policy change and scaling action writes an attributable audit row, so
an operator can reconstruct \emph{why} the system acted. One qualification, taken up
in \cref{disc:weaknesses}: the active policy version is persisted to a mutable
\texttt{policy.yaml} that can be edited out of band, so the audit row and the on-disk
policy can drift, and the trail is complete only as far as that file is treated as
immutable. The whole is held up by roughly seven hundred and eighteen automated tests
spanning unit, contract, and integration layers, which is what makes the
configuration choices below safe: switching an engine to its baseline is a tested code
path, not an act of faith.

\paragraph{Pluggable engines and load-balancer adapters.} The architecture is
extensible by design. Each decision service is a thin run loop hosting a swappable
engine behind a uniform contract, and the data plane is reached through a
load-balancer adapter interface. The strength this buys is \emph{reversibility}:
because every learned engine is one plugin behind a deterministic baseline, the
project can ship trained models while keeping production behaviour conservative,
promoting a model when it earns its place and returning to the baseline in one line of
configuration. That reversibility, more than any single model's score, is the
implementation result the evaluation most strongly supports, and it makes every
decision deterministically safe: whatever the learned engine proposes, the baseline
beneath it guarantees a predictable outcome.

\subsection{Design Trade-offs and Future Directions}
\label{disc:weaknesses}

\paragraph{Production routing is the deterministic heuristic; the trained policy adds
nothing today.} Production traffic is routed by the deterministic \texttt{monotone}
heuristic, not a learned model. That heuristic is the strong contender in the
closed-loop routing bench: on a homogeneous workload it holds the SLA-violation rate
to \BMrlMonoSla{} against \BMrlRrSla{} for round-robin, and it passes the monotonicity
probe a sane router must. The trained PPO policy is the \emph{worst} contender in the
same bench, at a \BMrlPpoSla{} SLA-violation rate, and on the older open-loop
evaluation it is round-robin-equivalent, tying plain NGINX round-robin to the digit
(\texttt{mean\_reward}$=$~\BMrlPpoReward{}, SLO-violation $0$). Credit for the routing
result therefore belongs to the monotone heuristic and to classical routing, not to a
policy that learned anything: the PPO model is dormant, contributes nothing to live
routing, and would degrade the loop if promoted. A discriminating learned policy could
in principle pull ahead on the more varied workload once trained on
heterogeneous-latency traces, the named next direction. Until then the trained router
stays in shadow mode, producing an observable, auditable decision stream at no cost to
the production path.

The project treated the tie as a result to diagnose rather than a sentence to leave.
The trained policy is \emph{round-robin-equivalent}, a byte-for-byte tie on every
offline metric, and four distinct root causes explain why. \Cref{tab:disc:rootcause}
sets them out. The finding is a mature diagnosis, not a failure: a policy that
converges to the optimal action on its training distribution has learned correctly,
and naming the four constraints that flatten the signal turns the result into a precise
specification for the next iteration.

routing and scaling are independent concerns.

Together these make the routing story precise. The trained model ties its baseline
because the workload gave it nothing to learn, and the one place SmartLoad is expected
to be materially slower is a known cost in the reload path, not a routing decision.
Both point at the conclusion the safety discipline already enforces: keep classical
routing in production until a model trained on discriminating data earns its place.

\paragraph{The harmonic-residual forecaster is the strongest engine and the deployed
default.} The forecasting path works end to end and drives the autoscaler correctly.
The shipped default, the \texttt{harmonic\_residual} predictor, is also the strongest
engine on the synthetic forecasting bench at \BMfcMape{} overall MAPE (with \BMfcCiCov{}
confidence-interval coverage), ahead of the naive baseline at \BMfcMapeNaive{},
\texttt{arima\_serving} at \BMfcMapeArima{}, and a plain moving average at \BMfcMapeMA{}.
The advantage carries downstream: in the autoscaling-downstream bench it lifts SLA
attainment by \BMfcSlaGain{} percentage points over the reactive baseline, closing
about a third of the reactive-to-oracle gap, the real downstream win a forecaster is
meant to deliver. A trained ARIMA$(2,0,2)$ model is wired behind a configuration flag,
but it fits out of distribution on the synthetic series and lands behind the deployed
engine, so it stays an option. This is the pluggable design working as intended: the
strongest engine ships as the default, with alternatives ready behind the same contract.

A second strategy-bench finding reinforces the mechanism-over-model thesis. The shipped
moving-average signal carries no forward projection, so under the same controller the
moving-average and reactive strategies land on identical SLA attainment, at
\BMasSlaReactive{} to the digit across every profile. The lever that moves that number
is the actuation chain, not the model: swapping the step-wise scaler for the
\texttt{target} controller lifts the same signal to \BMasSlaTarget{}, and only with a
genuine forward forecast under that controller does the trend strategy edge past
reactive control. The improvement comes from the control loop, not a more sophisticated
predictor.

\paragraph{The deterministic trend rule is the anomaly default, with the threshold
rule as its artefact-free fallback and the Isolation Forest as a selectable
alternative.} The deployed anomaly default is the deterministic, stateful
\texttt{trend\_rule} engine, chosen because it catches the gradual degradation
($F_1 =$~\BManFone{}) that every stateless detector misses. The artefact-free
\texttt{threshold} rule takes over automatically if no learned artefact is present. The
Isolation Forest is offered for operators who want a more aggressive detector: on the
threshold-versus-Isolation-Forest sweep the two agree on about \BManAgree{} of the grid,
and every disagreement has the Isolation Forest reporting unhealthy where the threshold
rule reports healthy, so it trades over-reaction for sensitivity. Comparing these
engines across the whole operating range is what makes that choice evidenced rather than
hopeful. As an extra margin, a quorum guard in the sidecar always keeps at least one
healthy backend in rotation, protecting every engine equally. The pattern is consistent
with the rest of the system: the shipped default is the engine that demonstrably wins on
the failure that matters, with a deterministic rule beneath it.

\paragraph{A focused single-node, single-tenant scope is a clean base for the
roadmap.} Several boundaries are deliberate scope choices for this phase. The canonical
deployment is a single-host Docker Compose stack, which keeps the system simple to run
and reason about; cross-host network behaviour, meaning real jitter, packet loss, and
multi-availability-zone latency, is the natural next thing to exercise. Portability
beyond this host is scaffolded rather than built. The Kubernetes path exists as a
\texttt{KubernetesClusterClient} that is named but not yet implemented, and the
alternative load-balancer adapters for ALB, Envoy, and HAProxy currently raise
\texttt{NotImplementedError}, so the NGINX data plane is the only working actuation path
today. Targeting a cloud or Kubernetes environment therefore means \emph{building} an
actuation path, not flipping a flag, and the comparison against the Kubernetes
Horizontal Pod Autoscaler \cite{k8shpa} comes within reach only once that path exists.
Each item is named, owned by a tracked issue, and, for multi-tenancy and
authentication, an explicit Phase-2 direction. The focused scope keeps the present
system small and clear and is a clean base for the planned multi-tenant and
Kubernetes-packaged roadmap.

\paragraph{Model operations, configuration drift, and control-plane-only health are
the honest gaps.} Three further limitations belong on record. The first is model
operations: trained engines are promoted by hand, with no content hash or manifest
pinning the artefact a service loads, no version-skew check between a model and its
serving code, and no automated retraining or drift-detection pipeline, so keeping the
learned layer current is a manual discipline. The second is configuration drift: the
active policy version lives in a mutable \texttt{policy.yaml} that can be edited out of
band, so the on-disk policy and the audit trail can diverge; the complete-audit-trail
claim holds only while that file is treated as immutable, and closing the gap means
moving policy state behind the same append-only discipline the audit log has. The third
is the health surface: several \texttt{/health} endpoints report control-plane liveness
and can return $200$ while the data plane is unservable, the exact gap that masked the
original $502$ outage until the end-to-end probe caught it, so the bounded-failure-modes
guarantee is only as strong as the data-plane probes behind it. Naming these keeps the
readiness picture honest.

\section{Economic Impact and Sustainability}
\label{disc:economic}

SmartLoad's economic case rests on two pillars: a cost structure that favours the
customer it targets, and a path by which the project sustains itself after the
present academic phase concludes.

\subsection{Cost-to-benefit structure}
\label{disc:cost_benefit}

The intended customer, established in \cref{ch:intro}, is the engineering team
\emph{below} the threshold at which a full Kubernetes control plane and the staff to
run it can be justified: a team running a backend pool on a handful of hosts that needs
adaptive traffic management but cannot absorb the operational overhead of
orchestration. Their realistic alternatives are to over-provision permanently, paying
continuously for idle capacity sized to peaks that arrive rarely, or to hand-assemble a
load balancer, a separate autoscaler, a monitoring stack, and an alerting tool, each
with its own learning curve and none sharing a decision surface. The autoscaling
literature has tracked this over-provisioning-versus-reactivity trade-off for over a
decade \cite{lorido2014autoscaling}, and managed autoscalers such as the Kubernetes
Horizontal Pod Autoscaler \cite{k8shpa} address it only for teams that have already
paid the orchestration entry cost.

SmartLoad's cost structure targets this gap. It is open-source and self-hosted, with no
per-node or per-core licensing fee, the dominant recurring cost of commercial
application-delivery controllers. It runs entirely on commodity, widely-audited
dependencies (an NGINX data plane, a Redis control bus, a TimescaleDB store, and the
standard Python scientific stack), so it introduces no exotic infrastructure a small
team would struggle to operate. Its central technical benefit is itself economic:
scaling the pool \emph{down} when demand recedes turns the over-provisioning premium
into right-sized spend. The main cost is operational, since a team must run and watch
one more piece of middleware, which is exactly the cost the single-unit container
manifest is designed to keep small. \Cref{tab:disc:cost_benefit} summarises the
trade-off.

\subsection{Sustainability after the project}
\label{disc:sustainability}

A graduation prototype abandoned at submission has no impact, so how SmartLoad sustains
itself beyond this phase is part of its design. Two complementary routes are open.

The first is the open-source self-hosted model. With no licensing dependency and
commodity infrastructure, the system can be adopted, forked, and maintained by any team
that finds it useful at no marginal cost to the originating effort, and the single-unit
container manifest, complete audit trail, and deterministic fallback lower the barrier
to that adoption. A cautious team can run the most conservative configuration, classical
routing with learned components in shadow, and gain the autoscaling and observability
benefits without ever trusting a model in the request path. An open tool useful in its
safest configuration can persist on its utility alone.

The second is a managed-SaaS path, scoped as a future Phase 2. The capabilities a
multi-customer offering needs, including multi-tenancy, tenant API keys and role-based
access control, per-token rate limiting, operator authentication, and TLS termination,
are planned for after the present single-tenant phase and tracked as distinct work. The
architecture was scoped \emph{toward} that path without prematurely paying for it: the
control plane, the policy surface, and the decision-envelope contracts already have the
shape a managed offering would need, so the SaaS direction is a credible route rather
than a rewrite. The present work is sustainable as middleware a team runs itself; the
managed control plane is the plausible later route to sustaining it as a product.

\section{Societal Impact}
\label{disc:societal}

The reliability of online services has crossed from convenience into welfare. People
book medical appointments, sit examinations, file taxes, and reach emergency
information through web applications, and a sustained slowdown or outage during a demand
peak is a genuine denial of service. It is felt most sharply at the high-load moments
that matter most, such as a results page opening at a fixed hour or a benefits portal
under a filing deadline. A system that grows capacity \emph{before} a predictable peak,
rather than scrambling after the alarm fires, improves the experience of everyone
waiting behind that front door, most of all at the moments of highest collective stake.
The benefit is concrete: more reliable backend pools mean fewer of the failures that,
at scale, become missed appointments, failed submissions, and people unable to reach a
service they depend on.

The subtler contribution is the project's safety-first posture toward automation in the
request path. The recent history of operational software shows a recurring pattern: an
automated control system given authority over a production resource and trusted to act
faster than a human can amplify a small error into a large outage precisely because it
acts confidently and at scale, and the danger is sharpest when the automation is opaque.
SmartLoad is designed against that failure mode. Every learned decision is logged and
attributable; every learned component has a deterministic rule behind it that takes over
on failure; and a single operator flag collapses all learned behaviour back to classical
routing without taking the system offline. The learned router ships pinned to a passive
shadow mode in which it records what it \emph{would} do but cannot move live traffic
until an operator promotes it. The claim is modest and defensible: a tool that places
automation in the request path while guaranteeing a deterministic fallback and an
operator override on every decision reduces, rather than raises, the risk that opaque
automation causes the outages it was meant to prevent. The professional-ethics
literature frames exactly this obligation, that engineers hold the public's safety and
welfare paramount and avoid deploying systems whose failure modes they cannot bound
\cite{acm2018ethics, gotterbarn1999ethics}, and the fallback-and-override architecture
is how the project meets it.

\section{Environmental Impact}
\label{disc:environmental}

SmartLoad's environmental footprint has two phases: the cost of building and training
it, and the effect of running it in production. They point in opposite directions, and
a fair account holds both.

During development the footprint is modest. The system runs as a Docker Compose stack
on commodity hardware, within the envelope of a single developer workstation. The one
real energy cost is model training: fitting the routing policy, forecaster, and anomaly
model consumes compute, with routing the most demanding task at roughly two million
steps over about seventy-five minutes of CPU time. That cost is paid once, offline, and
spread across every later inference, since a trained artefact is reused indefinitely at
negligible per-decision cost. The training energy is a one-time investment, not a
recurring drain.

In production the intended effect is to \emph{reduce} energy use. The usual strategy for
surviving demand spikes is to leave spare capacity permanently on, with racks drawing
power and cooling for a load that arrives only occasionally. SmartLoad's autoscaler is
bidirectional: right-sizing the pool means scaling it \emph{down} when demand recedes,
not only up when it rises, and idle capacity switched off is energy not wasted. The
committed benchmark run demonstrated this, with the pool growing under a forecast burst
and shrinking again as the burst subsided rather than staying enlarged
(\cref{fig:imc:pool}). One caveat keeps the claim honest: the scale-out signal is the
forecast upper band, a proxy for offered rate rather than a measurement of true pre-shed
arrivals, so under heavy shedding the signal and real demand can diverge. Right-sizing
is sound as a direction, with the exact saving qualified by that load-confound. At one
small deployment the saving is modest; as a \emph{default behaviour} across many
deployments, scaling idle capacity down is a meaningful lever on the energy footprint of
everyday computing.

A fair account also notes the rebound effect. By lowering the marginal cost of compute,
a tool that makes capacity cheaper to provide can encourage teams to deploy more of it,
the classic efficiency rebound in which the per-unit saving is partly or wholly offset
by increased aggregate use. SmartLoad does not escape this, so the project claims no
guaranteed net reduction across a whole population of adopters. The precise claim is
narrower: for a fixed workload, right-sizing the pool down during troughs consumes less
energy than holding peak capacity continuously. Whether that per-deployment saving adds
up to a net benefit depends on adoption behaviour the tool does not control, and that
boundary is stated rather than glossed.

\section{Cultural Impact}
\label{disc:cultural}

Beyond budgets, services, and energy, SmartLoad changes how the people who run
infrastructure work, and this cultural effect is as deliberate as any technical
decision.

The first shift is the texture of on-call work. The conventional reactive model puts
operators in firefighting mode: an alarm fires once latency or utilisation has crossed a
threshold, and a human scrambles to add capacity or reroute traffic \emph{after} the
degradation is felt. A system that forecasts demand and acts ahead of the spike moves
the human's role from reactive intervention toward \emph{supervision} of an automation
that handles the routine case, close in spirit to the autonomic-computing vision of
self-managing systems that free operators from manual tuning \cite{kephart2003autonomic}.
The on-call engineer watches that the automation is behaving and steps in for the
exceptional case rather than being the first response to every surge, a real improvement
for a role whose burnout is a well-known industry problem.

The second shift is the trust the first depends on. Operators will not cede routine
response to an automation they cannot see into, and SmartLoad is built to be seen into:
a live decision feed shows every engine's verdict as it is made, and a complete audit log
records every policy change and scaling action so any decision can be reconstructed.
Transparency is the precondition for supervised automation, since an operator can
supervise only what is observable, and the system treats it as a first-class feature.

The third shift respects that appetite for autonomy is not uniform. How much control a
team will cede over a production resource varies enormously between individuals, teams,
and engineering cultures. Some promote a learned router the moment it shows value;
others, for reasons of regulation, risk tolerance, or institutional caution, will not
trust an opaque component near production traffic until they can inspect and reverse
every decision. A product demanding a single fixed level of trust would alienate one of
these audiences whichever it chose. SmartLoad makes autonomy a setting: the safe-mode
kill switch and shadow-mode default let a cautious team adopt the same product as an
aggressive one, each at the level of delegation it can defend to its own management. The
cultural impact is not that SmartLoad imposes a new way of working but that it
\emph{accommodates} the range of ways teams already feel about handing authority to
software, which is what lets it be adopted across very different cultures of caution.

commitment makes the economic case adoptable, the environmental saving safe to enable by
default, the societal benefit defensible against the failure modes of opaque automation,

intelligence and classical safety in the same system, and to be clear about which is
carrying the load today.
